PLATFORM
The board is the i.MX 8M Plus, so the NPU (2.3 TOPS) is available for AI alongside the GPU for real-time classical CV.

What each block can do
- VPU (Video Processing Unit): H.264/H.265/VP8/VP9 decode, H.264/H.265 encode. Cannot do any vision processing (keypoints, optical flow, warp).
- GPU (Vivante GC7000UL): OpenVX, OpenCL, OpenGL ES: pyramids, optical flow, corners, warp, per-pixel correction. Not efficient for large neural networks.
- NPU (2.3 TOPS): int8 neural networks via TFLite / VX delegate. Not for classical CV; some ops fall back to CPU.
- ISP (dual): demosaic, HDR merge, AE/AGC, denoise for Bayer sensors. Likely unused with raw ADC input.
- CPU (4x Cortex-A53): control logic, homography, small matrix math, focus motor control. Not for per-pixel work at 50 fps.

To clarify
- Answered: board chosen (i.MX 8M Plus); development can use equivalent 8M Plus boards (NXP EVK or a SoM dev kit) as long as the BSP matches the target.
- Answered: raw ADC output per the manufacturer's doc.
- Sensor model and type (thermal or visible), interface, bit depth and max frame rate.
- Output path: HDMI display, encoded stream (VPU), or both?
- BSP / Linux version, and are OpenVX and the NPU delegate in the image?

Accept
- VPU only for final encoding of the 1280x1024 output.
- GPU (OpenVX/OpenCL) for per-pixel classical CV.
- NPU for AI models, mainly a slower enhanced turbulence output.

Reject
- Running keypoints, matching or stabilization on the VPU: the hardware does not support it.


RAW ADC TO IMAGE (FIRST STAGE)
Every other topic starts from this stage: the sensor delivers raw ADC values, and we must turn them into a clean, linear image before stabilization, turbulence correction, focus or AGC can work.

What we have
- Raw ADC output from a 640x512 matrix; conversion to follow the manufacturer's doc.
- The i.MX ISP is likely unused, so this stage is our own code.

Likely steps (exact list depends on the sensor and the manufacturer's doc)
1. Unpack: bit depth, packing (for example 14-bit in 16-bit words), byte order, any header or telemetry lines in the frame.
2. Offset / black level: subtract the dark level.
3. Non-uniformity correction (NUC): per-pixel gain and offset from the manufacturer's calibration tables; for a thermal sensor, periodic flat-field correction (shutter).
4. Bad-pixel replacement: from the manufacturer's map, plus detection of pixels that fail later.
5. Colour pipeline, only if the sensor is visible Bayer: demosaic, white balance, colour correction. In that case the i.MX ISP may do it if the data arrives over MIPI CSI-2.
6. Light denoise (optional): only if the noise hurts stabilization or focus.
7. Hand-off: keep 16-bit linear data for stabilization, turbulence and focus; AGC converts to 8-bit at the end.

To clarify
- Sensor type (thermal or visible) and bit depth.
- Raw format: packing, byte order, header or telemetry lines.
- Interface: MIPI CSI-2, parallel, USB, Ethernet?
- What calibration data the manufacturer provides: NUC tables, bad-pixel map, per-unit or generic.
- Is there a shutter or other flat-field mechanism, and who triggers it?
- Per-frame telemetry: sensor temperature, frame counter, timestamps.

Accept
- Follow the manufacturer's correction procedure first, then improve only where needed.
- Run the per-pixel steps on the GPU (OpenCL) with zero-copy buffers.
- Keep linear 16-bit data until the final AGC stage.

Reject
- Converting to 8-bit early: it throws away the detail that turbulence correction, focus and AGC need.
- Building our own NUC calibration before trying the manufacturer's tables.


1. HOT-AIR DISTORTION (ATMOSPHERIC TURBULENCE)
This is the highest-risk item: classical methods exist and are cheap for static scenes, but moving objects and the 50 ms latency target make it hard on either path.

What we have
- Assumption that only AI can solve it. That is not quite right: classical methods exist.
- Reference model: Turb-Seg-Res (segment moving objects, then restore). Too heavy even on an NVIDIA 3070 laptop GPU.
- Lots of training data expected from the client.

Known classical approaches
- Register frames with dense optical flow, then temporal mean or median over past frames. Cheap, works well on static scenes.
- Lucky imaging / lucky region fusion: keep the sharpest regions across a window of past frames.
- Non-rigid registration followed by blind deconvolution (Zhu and Milanfar).

Training data for AI
- Real turbulent footage has no clean ground truth.
- Options: paired recordings (same static scene, calm vs hot air), synthetic turbulence applied to clean frames with a physics-based simulator, or self-supervised training on temporal consistency.
- Realistic plan: synthetic pairs for training, paired recordings for validation.

To clarify
- Typical distance to target and conditions (roads, summer heat)?
- Mostly static scene, or moving targets that must stay sharp?
- Is a slower (about 1 fps) enhanced output acceptable next to the 50 fps live view?
- Answered: no look-ahead (50 ms target); temporal methods may use past frames only.
- Sample footage with turbulence, ideally with and without moving objects.

Accept
- Classical causal temporal fusion (past frames only) with a motion mask: fused background, raw pixels for moving regions.
- A slower enhanced output (about 1 fps) on the NPU if the client accepts it.
- Profile Turb-Seg-Res or a reduced version on the NPU only after classical is measured.

Reject
- Claiming that no classical method exists.
- Running Turb-Seg-Res at full size at 50 fps on 2.3 TOPS.


2. STABILIZATION
Classical keypoint-based stabilization is the default: the per-pixel steps run on the GPU through OpenVX, the rest on the CPU, provided our footage has enough texture.

What we have
- No gimbal at this moment; we remove mount vibration. A gimbal may be added later.
- Node list taken from the NVIDIA VisionWorks video stabilizer sample.
- Idea to check keypoint density on the provided images before choosing classical vs AI.
- AI reference list: awesome-video-stabilization.

Where each node runs
- convert_to_gray, copy: standard OpenVX, GPU.
- pyramid: standard OpenVX, GPU.
- optical flow (pyramidal LK): standard OpenVX, GPU.
- feature_track (Harris / FAST): standard OpenVX, GPU.
- warp_perspective: standard OpenVX, GPU.
- find_homography: VisionWorks extension, not standard; CPU (OpenCV RANSAC).
- homography_filter, matrix_smoother, truncate_stab_transform: VisionWorks extensions, not standard; CPU (small 3x3 matrix math).

To clarify
- Answered: no gimbal at this moment, so we remove mount vibration; a gimbal may be added later.
- Answered: no look-ahead frames allowed (end-to-end under 50 ms).
- Mount type (fixed mast or vehicle), vibration amplitude and frequency.
- Does the camera move intentionally (manual pan, vehicle turns), and is a gimbal planned? The stabilizer must follow intended motion, not fight it.
- Acceptable border loss from cropping after warp (typically 5 to 10%).
- Typical scenes: is there low-texture content (sky, sea, haze) where keypoints disappear?

Accept
- Classical pipeline at 640x512, before turbulence fusion (fusion needs aligned frames).
- Causal smoothing (Kalman or exponential) for zero added latency; tuned to remove vibration while following intended camera motion, including a gimbal if one is added later.
- Measure per-frame feature count and RANSAC inlier ratio on real footage before committing.
- Fallback for low texture: phase correlation (translation only), no keypoints needed.

Reject
- Running stabilization on the VPU.
- The VisionWorks centered smoothing window as-is: it uses future frames, which the 50 ms target rules out.
- AI stabilization models at 50 fps on this hardware, unless classical fails on the data.


3. LENS FOCUS (NO NET)
There is no target in front of the camera, so focus is found from the scene itself: drive the focus motor, measure image sharpness, and take the motor position where sharpness peaks. This is classical contrast-detection autofocus; no AI needed.

What we have
- No net or target in front of the camera: the algorithm must work on the live scene.
- Goal: find the best-focus motor position (the peak of the focus curve) for the current zoom.
- Decision: a visible refocus is acceptable (refocus, then continue).
- Zoom is expected to be stepped; to be confirmed.

Algorithm
1. Start point: zoom-to-focus table from a factory sweep, so the search starts near the answer.
2. Sharpness metric: Tenengrad (Sobel gradient energy) or variance of Laplacian, normalized by image intensity so brightness changes do not move the peak. Computed in a central ROI, or several zones with the strongest one used.
3. Freeze AGC during the search: AGC changes contrast and would distort the curve.
4. Average out turbulence and noise: take the metric over several frames, or on the turbulence-fused frames, at each motor position.
5. Coarse-to-fine search: coarse steps across the range, then fine steps around the best point; golden-section search reduces the number of steps.
6. Sub-step peak: fit a parabola (or Gaussian) to the 3 to 5 best points and take its vertex.
7. Backlash: always approach the final position from the same direction.
8. Confidence check: if the curve is flat (low-contrast scene, fog, uniform sky), keep the table value instead of a noisy peak.
9. Triggers: zoom change, sustained sharpness drop, operator request.

To clarify
- By "focal center" do we mean the best-focus motor position (above)? If the optical center (principal point) is meant, that is a separate geometric calibration.
- Lens control interface: motor steps, range, speed, backlash, position feedback?
- How many zoom positions; stepped or continuous?
- How long may a refocus take (seconds)?
- Do the cameras stay stable over time, or is periodic recalibration needed?

Accept
- Contrast-detection autofocus as above, with the factory zoom-to-focus table as the starting point.
- Sharpness measured on several frames (or fused frames) per position, with AGC frozen.
- Parabola fit for a sub-step peak and a confidence check before moving the motor.

Reject
- AI-based focus estimation: unnecessary for this problem.
- A single-frame sharpness measurement: turbulence and noise make it unreliable.
- One shared calibration table for all units without verifying lens tolerance spread.


4. HDR AND AUTO GAIN CONTROL
With raw ADC input, this is our own algorithm work: mapping the high-bit-depth linear image to an 8-bit display image while keeping detail in both bright and dark areas.

What we have
- Raw ADC input; the i.MX ISP is likely unused, so the ISP's HDR and AE/AGC do not apply.
- HDR / automatic luminance: need to see the data.
- Gain auto control: no details yet.
- CLAHE demo (clahe_demo.py) for exploring local contrast enhancement.

To clarify
- Sensor type and bit depth (decides the mapping method).
- Does the sensor have its own gain or integration-time control we must drive?
- Which scenes are hardest: bright sky over a dark target, hot objects, night, backlight?
- Is there a region of interest the output must favour (center, tracked target)?
- Day/night operation?

Accept
- Baseline: histogram-based mapping (plateau equalization) from 16-bit linear to 8-bit, with temporal smoothing so brightness does not flicker.
- Local detail enhancement (CLAHE-style or base/detail split) on the GPU if global mapping loses detail.
- ROI-weighted mapping if a target region matters.
- AGC runs last in the pipeline, after stabilization and turbulence fusion.

Reject
- Converting to 8-bit before turbulence fusion and focus.
- Multi-frame software HDR at 50 fps: it doubles the sensor rate and adds ghosting on motion.


CROSS-CUTTING: LATENCY, ORDER, SHARED RESOURCES
At 50 fps each stage has 20 ms per frame; the under-50 ms end-to-end target leaves about 2.5 frames, so look-ahead is ruled out and every stage must be pipelined.

Pipeline order
Raw ADC (640x512) -> unpack / NUC / bad pixels -> stabilize -> turbulence fusion -> AGC/HDR -> x2 zoom (1280x1024) -> display / VPU encode
Focus metric taken from the fused frames -> focus motor.

Watch out for
- Look-ahead is ruled out: each future frame costs 20 ms of the 50 ms budget.
- The GPU is the most contested resource: correction, stabilization, warp, turbulence fusion, AGC and zoom all run there.
- The NPU is free for an AI turbulence model now that super-resolution is deprioritized.

To clarify
- Answered: under 50 ms end-to-end.
- Which features run at the same time, and which are operator modes?
- Power and thermal limits: fanless enclosure, ambient temperature?

Accept
- Pipelined stages with zero-copy buffers between them.
- A per-stage latency budget built from measurements on the board, against the 50 ms target.

Reject
- Committing to "all features on, 50 fps, minimal latency" before profiling the combination.
